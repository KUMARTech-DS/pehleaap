One link per guest family, from the wedding invite to the thank-you note.

Hindi for “after you”, the polite way to offer someone your turn.

Blessing+ Envelope marked 147+ Photo= the same 40 seconds on stage

If the system knows which family was on stage at each moment, everything from that moment links to that family. Nobody has to type a name.

The problem

Every wedding breaks in the same places

An Indian wedding runs over several days for hundreds of guests. The family manages it through WhatsApp forwards and a paper notebook.

Before the wedding

  • Gifts collide. Nobody knows what the couple needs or what anyone else is bringing, so gifts default to safe, old-fashioned choices. The running joke is the couple who receives seven pressure cookers.
  • Headcounts are guesses. RSVPs are a casual “haan, aayenge”, so the caterer guesses plates for every function. Guess high and food is wasted. Guess low and the hosts are embarrassed.

On the day

  • The stage line. Every family wants a turn on stage to bless the couple, hand over the shagun envelope and get one photo. The line routinely runs 30–60 minutes or more.
  • Everyone pays for it. Elders stand through it and people cut in. Guests with a second wedding that night give up and leave. The couple stands for hours.

After the wedding

  • Nameless photos. The photographer has hundreds of stage photos and no names. Most guests never see their own photo with the couple.
  • The envelope bag. Envelopes say “Sharma family” (which Sharma?) or nothing, and gift tags fall off backstage. Late at night the family writes the lena-dena diary by hand, while UPI shagun sits scattered in someone's payment history.
  • No thank-yous. Nobody can face writing 400 messages.

Root cause

Every tool in the chain works on its own: the e-invite image, the WhatsApp group, the photographer's memory card, the envelope bag and the notebook. None of them knows who a guest is across the whole wedding, so the family connects everything by hand.

The solution

One record and one link per family

Pehle Aap gives every guest family one record and one link that follow them through the whole wedding. The page behind the link changes with the date.

How it's different

A ledger that fills itself

A shagun ledger normally needs someone to type every name. This one fills itself from the stage.

Names without faces

Wedding photo apps such as Kamero and Samaro use face recognition so guests can find their own photos. Pehle Aap tells the couple who is in every stage photo, using only time.

An invite that becomes a pass

An e-invite is usually a static image or video. Here it's the guest's pass for the whole wedding.

Nothing to install

Guests need one link and one QR code.

Design principles

  • The browser does the heavy lifting. Reading photo timestamps, decoding the QR clock and resizing photos happen on the photographer's laptop. The server stores small metadata and does the matching.
  • The database is never exposed. All reads and writes go through server code. Row Level Security is on with no rules, so even Supabase's public key can't read or write a row.
  • Polling, not websockets. Banquet halls have poor signal. An open page asks for an update every 5 seconds, and a phone in a pocket gets push notifications. A dropped connection just means the next request catches up.
  • No accounts. Each role and each family gets an unguessable link. Guests live in WhatsApp, and a sign-up screen would lose most of them.
  • No money, no faces. UPI payments happen in the guest's own app, and photos are matched by time, not biometrics.

Data model

Nine tables. Everything hangs off family.

wedding

Couple, UPI ID, token counter, plate margin and the role links.

event

One per function. Whether it has a stage, and when the couple tapped Start.

family

One per guest family: link, side, elders, token, and the ledger amounts.

rsvp

Headcount per family per function.

checkin

On the day: arrived, leaving by, called, skipped, push subscription.

stage_log

Every call, skip, pause and resume, stamped by the stage phone. Safe to replay after the network drops.

gift

Wishlist items and who claimed them.

batch

One camera's upload and its clock offset.

photo

The camera's own time and the matched family.

Before the wedding

Digital invite and RSVP

  • Setup. The couple adds their names, a photo, the functions (date, time, venue, map link), the UPI ID for shagun, and the wishlist.
  • Shared link. Most guests come in through /i/<slug>, posted in family WhatsApp groups. They enter family name, phone and side, and land on their personal family link. The device remembers it, and the page tells them it's their pass on the wedding day.
  • Added by hand. The couple can add a family themselves, such as grandparents without smartphones, and send their link with one tap through wa.me.
  • RSVP per function. “How many of you are coming to the Sangeet? The reception?” One extra question feeds the queue: “Is anyone 75 or older?” Answers can change at any time.
  • One registration per number. If a number is already registered, the page points to the original link, which the couple can resend. Typing someone else's number never opens their page.
  • Looks like a card on WhatsApp. next/og generates the preview image from the couple's names, date and photo. Keep it small, because WhatsApp skips large preview images.

Before the wedding

Gift wishlist

  • Privacy. Guests see each item as available or taken, never who took it.
  • Claims can't collide. Claiming is one atomic statement, so two guests tapping the same item at the same moment can't both win.

  • Releasing claims. Guests can un-claim their own item. The couple can release any claim, for the guest who claimed and never bought.
  • Feeds the ledger. Claims appear under that family automatically. Even if the tag falls off the box backstage, the couple knows who brought the air fryer.

  • Before the day. For each function: confirmed headcount, families who haven't answered, and a suggested order of confirmed × (1 + margin). The couple sets the margin (10% by default), because every family's “haan, aayenge” means something different.
  • On the day. Every check-in records how many people actually came. The kitchen sees arrivals against the plan and the last 15 minutes, so it can see a rush coming. It's a minimum, because guests who never check in aren't counted.

select e.name,
       sum(r.headcount)                               as confirmed,
       ceil(sum(r.headcount) * (1 + w.plate_margin))  as plates_to_order
from rsvp r
join event e   on e.id = r.event_id
join wedding w on w.id = e.wedding_id
where w.id = $wedding
group by e.id, w.id
order by e.starts_at;

On the day

Check-in and the token queue

Arriving

  • Families with a link tap “We've arrived”, confirm how many came, and can add “we must leave by 10:30”.
  • Walk-ins scan the entrance QR (/here/<slug>) and enter family name, side and headcount. If the phone number matches a registered family, that family is checked in, and this phone shows only their token and place in line, not the full family page.
  • Guests without a smartphone are added by the stage manager, who tells them their number.
  • Token numbers never repeat. They come from an atomic counter, so two families checking in at the same second never share a number.
update wedding set next_number = next_number + 1
where id = $wedding
returning next_number - 1 as number;   -- the row lock lines up simultaneous check-ins

Who goes next: the due-time rule

Every family in line gets a due time, which is when they should ideally be on stage. The stage serves the earliest due time first.

const MIN = 60_000;
const DUE = { regular: 45, elder: 15, leaveBuffer: 10 }; // minutes; tune after the first real wedding

type InLine = { arrivedAt: Date; elders: boolean; leaveBy?: Date };

// Never earlier than arrival, so a family can only be overtaken by people who arrived before its due time.
function due(f: InLine) {
  const t = f.arrivedAt.getTime();
  const options = [t + DUE.regular * MIN];
  if (f.elders) options.push(t + DUE.elder * MIN);
  if (f.leaveBy) options.push(f.leaveBy.getTime() - DUE.leaveBuffer * MIN);
  return Math.max(t, Math.min(...options));
}

export const order = <T extends InLine>(line: T[]) =>
  [...line].sort((a, b) => due(a) - due(b) || +a.arrivedAt - +b.arrivedAt);

Order on the stage screen

    “Up” counts the places a family gains over plain arrival order.

    • Regular families all get the same 45 minutes, so among themselves it's plain first come, first served.
    • Elders. Nanaji's family arrives at 8:20 and is due at 8:35. They go ahead of regular families who arrived after 7:50, and behind everyone who arrived earlier.
    • Early leavers. A family that arrives at 9:10 and must leave by 9:40 is due at 9:30. They go ahead of regular families who arrived after 8:45.
    • Nobody waits forever. A due time is never earlier than the arrival time, so a family due at 9:00 can only be overtaken by families that arrived before 9:00. That group is finite, so every family gets called.
    • Human override. The stage manager can call any family directly (the grandparents, the boss) and can see who flagged themselves, which keeps the “leaving soon” flag honest.

    Estimated wait

    The time left in the current turn, plus the expected stage time of every family ahead. Expected stage time is a + b × headcount: a fixed part for greeting the couple and the envelope, and a part per person for fitting everyone into the photo. a and b are fitted by least squares from finished turns, starting at 30 s and 8 s so the first estimates make sense. The page shows “12th in line, about 25 min”, or “stage paused” during a break.

    Telling families when to come

    When the stage manager taps “Call next”, the called family gets “You're on!” and the family now third in line gets “Head towards the stage”. These go out as push notifications, which work in Chrome on Android, and on iPhone only once the page is added to the home screen. They also show on the page if it's open, and the venue TV shows the numbers for everyone else, along with the entrance QR for late arrivals.

    The stage screen

    • Call next. A big button, with the next five families and their flags underneath.
    • Skip. For a family that isn't there. They stay one tap away in a Skipped list, and the skipped call doesn't count as a turn.
    • Pause and Resume for the couple's dinner break, plus Add walk-in and Show QR clock.
    • Works offline. Every action is a stage_log row with an ID made on the device and the device's time. If the hall's network drops, actions wait on the device and are sent later. The ID means a resend can't create duplicates, and the order is kept.

    1. Calibrate. Before the line opens, the photographer uses each camera to photograph the QR clock on the stage screen. The QR holds the stage phone's current time, with large digits underneath as a fallback.

    2. Upload. Afterwards, or during a break, the photographer opens /upload/<key> on a laptop and picks one camera's folder.

    3. Read and correct, in the browser. exifr reads each JPEG's capture time (DateTimeOriginal) and jsQR finds the clock in the earliest photos. The page reports something like “This camera is 2 h 3 min behind.”

      // Read the camera's time as a plain number, as if it were UTC.
// One subtraction then covers clock drift, a wrong time zone and daylight saving.
const offsetMs = qrTimeMs - cameraMs(qrShot);

function familyFor(photo: Photo, turns: Turn[]) {
  const t = cameraMs(photo) + offsetMs;            // now on the stage clock
  return turns.find(s => s.start <= t && t < s.end)?.familyId ?? null;
}

    4. Resize and upload directly. The browser resizes each photo to 2,048 px on the long edge, so a 1,000-photo reception is about 400 MB instead of about 10 GB. Photos go straight to Supabase Storage through signed upload URLs. The photographer still delivers full-resolution originals separately.

    5. Match. A turn runs from one “call” to the next, or to a “pause” plus 10 seconds of grace for the “ek aur photo” uncle. A skipped call creates no turn. Photos outside every turn, such as décor or the couple alone, stay unmatched.

    6. Deliver. Family pages switch to “Your stage photos are ready”. The couple's album is searchable by name, with an unmatched pile and a way to move a photo to the right family.

    • Precision. Corrected timestamps are accurate to about a second, and a turn lasts 30–90 seconds, which leaves plenty of margin.
    • Forgot the QR photo? Tap any photo and pick the family in it. The offset then comes from the middle of that family's turn, which can be off by up to half a turn, so moving stray photos matters more.
    • JPEG only in v1. Professional cameras can save a JPEG alongside RAW, and an iPhone can be set to “Most Compatible”.

    Security and privacy

    What keeps guests safe

    Unguessable links

    Every family and role link contains 16 random bytes. The couple can regenerate any of them, for example if the stage link gets forwarded.

    No database access from the browser

    Row Level Security is on with no rules. Only server code holding the service-role key, kept in Vercel's environment variables, can read or write.

    Phone numbers aren't passwords

    Registering or checking in with a known number never opens that family's page.

    Private photos

    Photos live in a private bucket. Pages get signed links that expire after an hour.

    Need-to-know screens

    The public TV board shows numbers, not names. Only the couple sees amounts.

    Minimal data

    Names, phone numbers and headcounts. No face data, no card or bank details. Deleting the wedding deletes everything, photos included.

    Validated on the server

    Every server action checks its input (lengths, allowed values, phone format) and that the link it came from may make that change.

    Someone claims a gift and never buys itThe couple releases the claim.

    A called family isn't thereTap Skip. They stay one tap away, and that call doesn't count as a turn.

    For the team

    Finals demo, about four minutes

    1. Invite. The projector shows the invite QR. Judges scan it and RSVP as families. Two of them tap the same gift at once: one gets it, the other sees “just taken”.

    2. Arrive. The couple taps Start on the reception. Judges tap “We've arrived” and get tokens, and the kitchen view counts up.

    3. Queue. Flag one judge as “Nanaji, 82” and watch them move up, while everyone else keeps their order.

    4. Stage. Call three judges to the front and photograph them with a camera set two hours wrong. Photograph the QR clock first.

    5. Photos. Upload the card. “This camera is 2 h 0 min behind”, and every photo carries the judges' names. Their phones show “Your stage photos are ready”.

    6. Ledger and thanks. Type their tokens into the counting screen and open the ledger: “Nobody typed a name.” Then tap thank-you for one judge and let their WhatsApp buzz.

    Bring your own mobile hotspot. Conference Wi-Fi is the banquet-hall network problem in miniature.

    Scope

    What we build later, and what we never build

    Deliberately not building

    • Venue, décor and caterer marketplaces. WedMeGood and WeddingWire India already do this, it needs vendor data we don't have, and every generic wedding app has one.
    • Muhurat calculation. Families follow their pandit, traditions differ by region, and even published muhurat lists for November 2026 disagree.
    • Face recognition. Time does the matching, so there's no biometric data to store or leak.
    • Payments. UPI already works. We only make sure the token travels in the note.
    • Native apps. A link and a QR code mean nothing to install at the venue.
    • WhatsApp Business API or SMS. Approval, and India's DLT registration for SMS, take longer than a hackathon.

    After the hackathon

    • WhatsApp one-time codes, so a family can open their page on a new phone.
    • Import a bank or UPI statement and match UPI shagun to families from the notes.
    • Hindi and regional-language screens.
    • Special meal counts for the kitchen, such as Jain and vegetarian.
    • An “add to home screen” prompt, so iPhone guests get push notifications.

    Glossary

    Wedding terms used on this page

    Shagun
    A cash gift to the couple, traditionally in an envelope (often with a one-rupee coin) and increasingly sent by UPI.
    Lena-dena diary
    The family notebook of who gave what, used to return the gesture at other families' weddings.
    Functions
    The separate events of one wedding (Haldi, Mehendi, Sangeet, the wedding, the reception), often over several days, each with its own guest count.
    Stage line
    The queue to go on stage, bless the couple, hand over shagun and have a photo taken.
    UPI
    India's instant payment system. Every payment can carry a short note.
    “Haan, aayenge”
    “Yes, we'll come”: the RSVP that tells a caterer nothing.
    “Ek aur photo”
    “One more photo”: the reason stage turns run long.
    Pandit
    The family priest, who usually chooses the wedding date.
    Muhurat
    An auspicious date and time for the wedding.
    Dev Uthani Ekadashi
    The festival day that marks the start of the wedding season.
